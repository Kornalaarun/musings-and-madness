There is a kind of looking that is just scanning — a way of moving through a room, a street, a conversation, without anything actually landing. You come away having seen a great deal and noticed very little.

The opposite is harder to sustain. To notice requires a moment of arrest: the mind has to stop moving long enough for something to become specific. The colour of this particular light at this hour. The fact that the train doors open on the left today instead of the right. The way a person hesitates before answering.

---

Much has been written about attention as a resource — finite, depletable, worth rationing. That frame is useful but incomplete. Attention is also a skill, and skills can be developed. The muscle grows stronger the more it is used.

One exercise: spend five minutes describing a familiar object as though you had never seen it before. Not creatively — accurately. Note its dimensions, texture, the way shadows fall on it, what it is made of. You will almost certainly run out of things to say within thirty seconds, then discover, if you stay with it, that there is more than you thought.

---

What is the point of this? Not obviously useful. You will not perform better at your job because you noticed the watermark on a coffee cup. But noticing tends to generalize in unexpected ways. The habit of seeing things as they are, rather than as expected, turns out to be the same habit that catches a faulty assumption in an argument, or spots the one data point that doesn't fit the model.

Attention, carefully practised on small things, seems to become available for larger ones.
