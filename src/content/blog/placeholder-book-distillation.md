## The central idea

Kahneman proposes that thinking operates through two systems. System 1 is fast, automatic, associative — it fires without effort, pattern-matches on experience, and delivers verdicts before System 2 has assembled an argument. System 2 is slow, deliberate, effortful — the kind of thinking you notice as thinking. It can override System 1, but rarely does, because it is expensive and because System 1 is usually right enough.

The book is essentially a catalogue of where System 1 goes wrong and why System 2 fails to correct it.

---

## Ideas worth keeping

**Anchoring.** The first number you hear in a negotiation becomes a gravitational centre around which subsequent estimates cluster. This effect is embarrassingly robust — it works even when the anchor is obviously arbitrary.

**The availability heuristic.** We estimate frequency and probability by how easily examples come to mind. Things that are vivid, recent, or emotionally charged feel more common than things that are drab but equally prevalent. Plane crashes feel more dangerous than car trips; neither intuition tracks the statistics.

**Loss aversion.** Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please. This is not a quirk: it shapes pricing, negotiation, and risk tolerance in systematic ways. Kahneman and Tversky called the broader framework *prospect theory*.

**The planning fallacy.** Projects routinely overrun their estimates, even when the estimators know that projects routinely overrun their estimates. The cure Kahneman recommends is *reference class forecasting* — looking at what similar projects actually took, rather than reasoning from the project's own internal logic.

**Peak-end rule.** We remember experiences by how they felt at their most intense moment and at their end, not by their average quality or duration. This has counterintuitive implications: extending a mildly unpleasant experience with a less-unpleasant ending makes the memory of the whole thing better.

## Caveats

The replication crisis has touched some of these findings. The priming effects Kahneman describes — where brief exposures to words unconsciously shift behaviour — have proven especially difficult to replicate. He acknowledged this publicly and urged caution. The broader framework of System 1 and System 2 remains influential, but it is better understood as a useful metaphor than a precise neurological description.

Anyone who makes decisions, which is everyone. The book is long; if time is short, the sections on anchoring, loss aversion, and the planning fallacy earn their word count most clearly.
